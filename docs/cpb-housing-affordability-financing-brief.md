# CPB Housing Affordability Brief for Fair Squares

**Purpose:** translate recent CPB housing-market publications into a practical problem/solution brief for Fair Squares.

**Focus period:** 2024–2026, with emphasis on financing problems behind affordable housing supply.

**Prepared for:** Fair Squares — decentralised affordable housing fund concept.

**Date:** 2026-05-14

---

## 1. Executive summary

Recent CPB work points to a clear diagnosis: the Dutch housing crisis is not only a shortage of homes, but a financing and allocation problem created by tight land supply, slow planning, regulated affordability requirements, limited corporate/housing-association investment capacity, and household credit constraints.

The central tension is that affordable housing generally offers lower rents and lower returns, while construction requires large upfront capital and competes with other uses of land and capital. If affordability is enforced without solving the underlying financing gap, fewer homes may be built. If subsidies are added without land and planning discipline, they can leak into higher land prices rather than more homes.

This is exactly the gap Fair Squares can position itself around:

> **Fair Squares should not present itself merely as another housing investment vehicle. It should present itself as a mission-aligned capital layer that converts socially motivated investor capital into predictable, capped-return, affordability-preserving housing finance.**

The strongest CPB-aligned opportunity is to finance projects where there is real housing demand and social value, but where conventional capital is too expensive, public subsidy is limited or poorly targeted, and housing corporations/municipal actors face balance-sheet or timing constraints.

## 3. What CPB says about the core problem

### 3.1 More housing matters more than perfect targeting

The November 2024 CPB study on construction, affordability and demand-fit finds that households most under pressure in the market sector are often **middle-income households**: too wealthy for social rent, too constrained for affordable market-sector housing.

CPB’s strongest finding is practical: for these constrained households, **the number of homes built matters more than the exact type of homes built**. Where land is scarce, smaller homes can help more households directly; larger homes can help through moving chains, but only if enough land is available.

**Implication for Fair Squares:**
Fair Squares should avoid an overly narrow “perfect unit” thesis. Its capital should support projects that increase real unit count, especially in locations where smaller, efficient, affordable units can be delivered without waiting for large speculative developments.

### 3.2 Affordability policy can reduce availability if financing is not solved

CPB repeatedly describes the trade-off between **affordability** and **availability**. Rent regulation and affordability requirements lower housing costs for successful tenants, but they can also reduce the expected return for developers and landlords. If returns fall below required levels, projects are delayed, cancelled, or rental properties are sold instead of retained as rental stock.

This is especially important in the mid-rent and affordable-rent segment. The policy aim is socially desirable, but the financing stack becomes harder: lower rents mean lower debt capacity and lower investor appetite.

**Implication for Fair Squares:**
This is Fair Squares’ core wedge. The protocol can provide lower-cost, lower-return, mission-aligned equity or junior capital to make affordable rental projects viable without forcing market-rate returns.

### 3.3 Housing corporations have low return requirements but limited investment capacity

In _Perspectief op de woningmarkt_, CPB explains that different segments have different financing models:

- Free-market rental housing can access deep international capital markets, but investors demand relatively high risk-adjusted returns.
- Housing corporations accept lower returns because of their social mission, but they rely heavily on debt serviced by rental income.
- Lower rents reduce corporation cashflow, which limits borrowing capacity and therefore limits how many new homes they can build.

CPB frames this as a trade-off: a small corporation sector with very low rents for the most vulnerable, or a broader corporation sector with rents closer to market levels. Public support can reduce the tension, but it is politically and fiscally constrained.

**Implication for Fair Squares:**
Fair Squares can act as a complementary capital pool for housing corporations or similar mission-driven operators: not replacing them, but increasing project-level financing capacity while preserving affordability.

### 3.4 Household mortgage access has deteriorated sharply

The 2026 _Toegankelijkheidsmonitor Koopwoningmarkt_ is highly relevant. CPB reports that:

- In 2015, a median-income household could still finance a median owner-occupied home.
- By 2024, there was a gap of more than **€100,000** between the median purchase price and the maximum mortgage based on median income.
- Nearly **twice modal income** is now needed to buy the median home.
- The share of homes reachable by a median household’s borrowing capacity fell from **61% in 2015** to **21% in 2024**; in the four largest cities, only **18%** remained reachable.
- Wealth has become more decisive: savings, home equity and parental support increasingly determine access.

CPB also warns that simply relaxing lending standards may improve access for some households, but can also raise prices, debt and monthly costs. Structural improvement requires more supply and reducing price-inflating incentives.

**Implication for Fair Squares:**
Fair Squares should not frame the solution as helping households borrow more. That risks feeding price inflation. A better frame is: reduce the need for individual households to carry full ownership leverage by creating shared, capped-return, affordability-oriented housing capital.

### 3.5 Untargeted subsidies can leak into land prices

The January 2025 CPB publication on housing subsidies is directly relevant to Fair Squares’ funding model. CPB argues that broad, untargeted construction subsidies will often have limited supply impact in the current Dutch market because supply responds weakly to price increases. Due to land scarcity and planning restrictions, subsidies can become capitalised into higher land values.

CPB does identify cases where subsidies are more promising:

- Projects that are otherwise financially unviable because regulated rents do not cover construction costs.
- Projects where municipalities lack funds for required public infrastructure.
- Temporary support during downturns to prevent viable projects from being cancelled.
- Targeted support for changing the composition of housing stock, including smaller units, splitting buildings, topping up existing buildings, or transforming existing structures.
- New-build-specific support rather than general housing demand subsidies.

**Implication for Fair Squares:**
Fair Squares should avoid becoming a generic subsidy-like capital source. It should deploy capital only where it closes a documented viability gap and where affordability obligations are locked in before land values adjust upward.

### 3.6 Land and spatial restrictions are binding constraints

The April 2025 CPB publication _Beschermde regio’s bouwen minder_ shows that around half of Dutch land is protected in some form. Regions with more protected land have lower housing-supply elasticity: when demand rises, construction responds less, scarcity increases, and affordability worsens.

CPB stresses this is a political trade-off: easing protection can help housing goals, but may harm nature, heritage, landscape, infrastructure or other public goals.

**Implication for Fair Squares:**
Fair Squares cannot solve planning law. It should therefore prioritise capital-efficient projects that do not rely primarily on new greenfield land: transformation, adaptive reuse, optopping, splitting, densification near existing infrastructure, and partnerships where zoning/planning risk is already reduced.

## 4. Financing problems Fair Squares can credibly solve

### Problem 1 — The affordable-rent viability gap

Affordable rental projects often fail because achievable rents do not support the required return on total development cost. Private capital asks for market returns; regulated rents cap income; public subsidy is uncertain or slow.

**Fair Squares solution angle:**
Create a capped-return capital pool that accepts lower financial upside in exchange for measurable social impact: below-market rents, long-term affordability covenants, and transparent project economics.

**Possible product:**
- Fair Squares Affordable Housing Notes
- Target return: capped, e.g. 2–4% net depending on risk and subsidy stack
- Capital use: junior equity, mezzanine, or preferred equity filling the gap between senior debt and total project cost
- Covenant: rent discount and affordability period encoded contractually, with on-chain reporting where possible

### Problem 2 — Housing corporations’ balance-sheet constraint

Corporations can operate at lower returns, but lower rents limit their borrowing capacity. Their pipeline may be constrained even when projects are socially desirable.

**Fair Squares solution angle:**
Provide off-balance-sheet or project-level co-investment capital alongside housing corporations, municipalities, or non-profit managers.

**Possible product:**
- Project SPV co-owned by Fair Squares investors and a housing corporation/operator
- Corporation handles tenant allocation and operations
- Fair Squares receives capped distributions
- Residual affordability value remains with the social mission rather than being extracted as speculative upside

### Problem 3 — Subsidy leakage into land prices

CPB warns that broad subsidies can inflate land values when supply is constrained. This means public money or mission capital may benefit landowners instead of households.

**Fair Squares solution angle:**
Only invest after land/control terms are fixed, or where the investment is tied to existing assets rather than speculative land acquisition.

**Possible safeguards:**
- Require land price or purchase option to be fixed before Fair Squares capital commitment
- Require open-book development budgets
- Prioritise transformation/optopping/splitting over land bidding wars
- Use clawbacks if affordability covenants are broken
- Publish project-level use-of-funds and valuation updates

### Problem 4 — Middle-income households trapped between social rent and market housing

CPB identifies middle-income households as often constrained: too high-income for social rent, too low-income for affordable market-sector housing.

**Fair Squares solution angle:**
Target the “missing middle” with rental housing that is neither traditional social housing nor fully market-rate.

**Possible model:**
- Income-screened mid-rent units
- Rent set at a fixed discount to local market rent or capped against income bands
- Investor return capped so rental affordability is not competed away
- Allocation handled by trusted local housing partners, not by speculative bidding

### Problem 5 — Individual households increasingly need wealth, not just income

The 2026 CPB accessibility monitor shows that buying increasingly depends on parental wealth, home equity and savings. This creates intergenerational inequality.

**Fair Squares solution angle:**
Shift from individual leveraged ownership to pooled ownership of affordable housing assets.

**Possible model:**
- Tenants rent affordably without needing a large down payment
- Social investors provide capital and receive capped income
- Optional long-term tenant participation: savings accounts, rent-to-equity, or community share mechanisms, but only if these do not reintroduce leverage and speculation

### Problem 6 — Planning uncertainty and slow supply response

CPB stresses that unpredictable demand and slow planning make the market fragile. Frequent policy changes can harm the investment climate.

**Fair Squares solution angle:**
Finance projects that are already far enough through planning to be executable, and create standardised funding templates that reduce transaction time.

**Possible product:**
- “Ready-to-build affordability facility” for projects with permits/zoning largely resolved
- Standardised term sheet for municipalities and housing operators
- Milestone-based drawdowns tied to permitting, construction start, completion and occupancy

## 5. Recommended Fair Squares positioning

### Core narrative

> The Dutch housing crisis needs more than higher mortgages or generic subsidies. Affordable homes require patient, mission-aligned capital that can accept capped returns, close project viability gaps, and keep affordability locked in over time.

### Suggested one-liner

> **Fair Squares is a transparent, capped-return capital layer for affordable housing projects that are socially valuable but underfinanced by traditional markets.**

### What to avoid saying

Avoid claiming that Fair Squares alone can “solve the housing crisis.” CPB is clear that land-use restrictions, planning, subsidies, household finance and sector interactions all matter. Fair Squares should instead claim a narrower and more credible role:

- unlock specific affordable projects;
- reduce dependence on high-return private capital;
- complement housing corporations and municipalities;
- prevent affordability subsidies from becoming speculative upside;
- create transparent social-investor participation.

## 6. Concrete solution concepts

### 6.1 Capped-return affordable housing fund

A pooled fund where verified investors accept capped returns. Returns above the cap are retained for affordability, maintenance, reserves, or expansion.

**Why CPB-aligned:**
It directly addresses the lower-return nature of affordable housing without relying only on public subsidy or high market rents.

### 6.2 Viability-gap financing facility

A facility that provides the final layer of capital for projects that are socially desirable but financially short under affordable rent caps.

**Best suited for:**
- regulated or mid-rent projects;
- projects with fixed land terms;
- projects with municipal/housing-corporation partners;
- transformation or densification projects.

### 6.3 Transformation and optopping finance

Capital targeted at converting, splitting, topping up or reusing existing buildings.

**Why CPB-aligned:**
CPB notes that changing the composition of the housing stock through splitting, topping up and transformation can add housing without requiring much additional land.

### 6.4 Municipality + operator + Fair Squares partnership

A three-party structure:

1. Municipality reduces planning/land uncertainty and may provide targeted support.
2. Housing corporation or professional operator manages tenants and buildings.
3. Fair Squares provides capped-return capital and transparent reporting.

**Why CPB-aligned:**
It separates the roles that CPB shows are often bundled inefficiently: public planning, social allocation, operational management and capital provision.

### 6.5 Anti-speculation covenant stack

Each project should include enforceable safeguards:

- affordability period;
- rent cap or discount formula;
- resale/refinancing limits;
- open-book development costs;
- capped investor returns;
- public reporting of aggregate project performance;
- clawback or penalty if affordability commitments are removed.

**Why CPB-aligned:**
It reduces the risk that subsidies or mission capital capitalise into land values or private windfalls.

## 7. Strategic fit with the existing Fair Squares prototype

The current Fair Squares Noir/Aztec prototype already maps well to this thesis:

- It pools capital from verified social investors.
- It targets affordable housing rather than speculative real estate.
- It uses capped returns, previously framed around roughly 3% annual yield.
- It separates public aggregate fund information from private investor information.
- It supports governance and transparent fund-level operations.
- It can use privacy-preserving KYC/eligibility without exposing individual investment sizes.

The protocol should, however, de-emphasise “DeFi yield” as the main story. CPB’s housing-market problem is not lack of generic yield. The real issue is **patient, low-cost, accountable capital for projects where affordability requirements suppress conventional returns**.

A better framing:

> DeFi or treasury yield may improve capital efficiency while funds wait to be deployed, but the core impact comes from financing affordable homes that conventional capital will not finance at affordable rents.

## 8. Practical project selection criteria

Fair Squares should prioritise projects with the following characteristics:

1. **Additionality:** the project is unlikely to proceed, or unlikely to remain affordable, without Fair Squares capital.
2. **Fixed land/control terms:** land value cannot simply rise in response to the availability of mission capital.
3. **Planning maturity:** zoning/permitting risk is reduced enough that capital can translate into homes.
4. **Affordability lock:** below-market rent or income-linked rent is contractually enforceable.
5. **Unit efficiency:** smaller, efficient units where land is scarce; mixed unit sizes only where larger units support genuine moving chains.
6. **Operator quality:** professional housing management, ideally with a housing corporation or mission-aligned operator.
7. **Transparent economics:** open-book budget, sources and uses, debt terms, reserves and return waterfall.
8. **No hidden speculation:** capped returns and limited refinancing/resale upside.

## 9. Risks and open questions

### Regulatory / legal

- Tokenised real-estate participation may trigger securities, AIFMD, MiCA or national financial-supervision issues.
- KYC and investor eligibility must be designed with legal counsel.
- Tenant allocation and housing regulation require local compliance.

### Economic

- Capped returns may not attract enough capital unless the social-impact proposition is strong.
- Construction cost inflation can erase viability gaps even after funding.
- Interest-rate changes can affect project debt capacity.

### Housing-market impact

- If Fair Squares capital competes for scarce land, it could unintentionally bid up prices.
- If affordability covenants are weak, value can leak to landowners, developers or future buyers.
- If projects are too small, impact may be credible locally but not systemically meaningful.

### Operational

- Property acquisition and management are not software problems; the protocol needs strong real-world operators.
- Governance must avoid slow decision-making that makes projects miss deadlines.
- Valuation, maintenance reserves and tenant-risk reporting need conservative standards.

## 10. Recommended next steps

1. **Rewrite Fair Squares pitch around the CPB financing gap.**  
   Move from “privacy-first housing fund” to “capped-return affordable housing capital layer.”

2. **Create a one-page investment thesis.**  
   Explain the CPB-backed problem: affordability lowers returns; corporations have limited balance sheets; generic subsidies leak into land; households cannot simply borrow more.

3. **Design a pilot term sheet.**  
   Include capped return, affordability covenant, project eligibility criteria, reporting, and investor protections.

4. **Identify 2–3 pilot project types.**  
   Best first candidates: transformation, optopping, splitting, or a municipality/corporation project with a clear viability gap.

5. **Map legal structure before token mechanics.**  
   Decide whether Fair Squares is a fund, cooperative, bond/note issuer, SPV platform, or protocol layer serving regulated entities.

6. **Add impact metrics to the prototype.**  
   Suggested metrics: homes financed, affordability discount, years affordability locked, public subsidy leveraged, cost per affordable unit unlocked, carbon/renovation metrics, and social return per euro of capped investor return.

## 11. Short conclusion

The CPB publications support a strong but disciplined Fair Squares thesis: the Netherlands needs more housing, but affordable housing often fails at the financing layer because lower rents reduce returns and borrowing capacity. Generic subsidies and looser mortgage credit can worsen prices if supply is constrained. Housing corporations have the right mission but limited balance-sheet capacity.

Fair Squares can credibly help if it provides patient, capped-return, transparent capital for projects where affordability is contractually locked and where the capital closes a real viability gap instead of inflating land prices.

The product should therefore be designed less like speculative real estate tokenisation and more like a transparent, programmable, social-impact financing layer for affordable housing.
